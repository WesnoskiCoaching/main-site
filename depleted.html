A Wesnoski Coaching guide

Depleted

How medication, nutrient status and digestion fit into the Wesnoski coaching review.

A medication list belongs beside the food history, symptoms and bloodwork. Our drug-nutrient framework asks what each medicine may change, what is relevant to the individual and how to support the person while their prescriber manages the medication. The ten sections below explain the areas we review and the types of support we consider. They are not ten automatic supplement stacks.

01 of 10

Statins: the mevalonate and mitochondrial connection

We start with the mevalonate pathway because it is involved in both cholesterol and CoQ10 production. CoQ10 participates in the mitochondrial electron-transport system, which is why we consider it when reviewing energy and muscle symptoms alongside statin use.

We establish the timing of any new symptoms, the actual medication, other medicines, training demands and relevant results. Muscle symptoms and fatigue have several possible causes; the pathway alone does not establish that CoQ10 depletion explains them.

The support discussion may include CoQ10, diet and the broader mitochondrial foundation. We also consider magnesium and other nutrients, selecting them according to the individual rather than assumed to be depleted in every statin user.

The plan should state what improvement is being monitored and when it will be reviewed. Do not stop a statin or substitute a supplement because of this guide. New or marked muscle symptoms belong in the medication review as well as the coaching history.

02 of 10

Metformin: B12 and the wider energy picture

B12 is the central nutrient connection when we review metformin use. Its roles include nerve function and red-blood-cell formation. Fatigue or tingling therefore deserves context rather than being automatically assigned to either diabetes or nutrient status.

We consider B12 with the blood count and, where appropriate, methylmalonic acid and homocysteine. Folate is considered alongside the wider methylation and dietary picture. These markers answer different questions and need to be interpreted together.

We consider B12 replacement where indicated, its form and route, and the ability to absorb and follow the plan. We may also consider CoQ10 for mitochondrial support; it is not a requirement for everyone taking metformin.

Keep track of the chosen support, energy, symptoms and repeat results. Neurological symptoms need timely assessment. The useful coaching contribution is consistent implementation and a connected review, not automatically adding every nutrient named here.

03 of 10

Acid blockers: digestion and absorption

Stomach acid has roles in protein digestion and the handling of some nutrients. Our framework therefore reviews long-term acid suppression alongside the reason for treatment, food intake, digestive symptoms and relevant B12, iron or mineral findings.

PPIs and H2 blockers are different medicines. Record the exact product and duration rather than treating them as interchangeable. Reflux symptoms alone cannot tell us that acid production is low.

Support can include food choices, meal routine, selected nutrient replacement and digestive support where appropriate. The iron guide explains why formulation and timing matter. The gut guide explains stomach acid, enzymes, bile and motility in more detail.

Betaine HCl is not something to layer onto an acid-suppressing prescription as a home experiment. Relevant exclusions and coordination with your physician matter. The coaching work can still improve the meal routine and address demonstrated needs while medication decisions remain with the prescriber.

04 of 10

Hormonal contraception: method first

Pills, injections, implants and IUDs do not all act in the same way. The nutrient and cycle discussion should begin with that distinction.

We review B vitamins, magnesium, zinc, selenium, CoQ10 and the copper context. Their roles span energy, blood cells, methylation, tissue maintenance and thyroid-related processes. Being on contraception does not, by itself, prove an individual deficiency in all of them.

We connect the method with symptoms, diet, bleeding, digestion and relevant results. A copper IUD, for example, belongs in a different discussion from a combined pill; bleeding and iron may be particularly relevant to the former.

The companion Birth Control guide explains the five-stage coaching framework and support options. A personalized plan should fit the method and goals rather than place every contraceptive user on the same stack.

05 of 10

SSRIs and SNRIs: nutrition, sleep and medication context

We review folate, magnesium, zinc and sleep-related concerns alongside these medications. The purpose is to keep nutritional status and recovery in the picture when someone reports fatigue, disturbed sleep or an incomplete response.

The medication name alone does not establish that a particular nutrient explains mood symptoms. We review dietary intake, symptom timing, existing results, other medicines and what has already been tried.

Selected nutritional support may be part of that plan, but adding several products at once makes both benefit and adverse effects harder to interpret. The intended purpose of each addition and its compatibility with the medication should be clear.

Sodium changes are a separate medical concern, not an instruction to add salt. Do not change an antidepressant dose or taper from this guide. The coaching work focuses on a sustainable food and recovery routine and the individually relevant nutrient picture.

06 of 10

NSAIDs: loss and the gut lining

Our framework distinguishes two questions with regular NSAID use: whether there is gastrointestinal injury or blood loss, and whether there are nutritional needs to address. Falling ferritin can be particularly relevant when a person also has heavy menstrual losses.

Record the actual frequency and reason for use, digestive symptoms and the iron trend. A supplement may be taken consistently while ongoing loss continues to undermine the result. That requires attention to the cause as well as replacement.

We consider mucosal-support options including zinc carnosine, DGL and glutamine in selected phases. These options do not protect against continued bleeding or replace an assessment.

Black, tar-like stool, visible blood, faintness or severe pain needs medical attention. For the coaching plan, connect the iron routine, digestion, food tolerance and recovery rather than adding another bottle.

07 of 10

Corticosteroids: bone, muscle and glucose

Our corticosteroid review includes bone-related nutrition, glucose regulation, muscle function and recovery. Dose, duration and the reason for treatment change the picture.

We review vitamin D, calcium, magnesium and other nutrients. The practical plan starts by establishing actual intake, relevant results and existing clinical monitoring. A list of possible nutrient concerns is not an automatic high-dose replacement plan.

Meals, suitable resistance work, recovery and the metabolic pattern remain relevant while the medicine continues. Where fatigue or weakness is present, the medication and underlying condition need to remain part of the interpretation.

Vitamin-D support is selected against the relevant results and monitoring plan. The amount should not be copied from another person’s protocol or a generic medication list.

08 of 10

Cardiovascular medicines: separate the classes

Beta blockers, ACE inhibitors and diuretics should not be treated as one depletion pattern. We consider CoQ10 with beta blockers, zinc with some ACE-inhibitor concerns, and electrolytes or thiamine in selected diuretic contexts.

The exact drug matters. Some medicines can lower potassium, while others can raise it. Kidney function, the rest of the prescription list and the monitoring plan influence what support is appropriate.

Fatigue, cramping or changes in exercise tolerance belong in a connected history. They do not identify a particular deficiency on their own. We review food intake, activity, symptoms and relevant results before selecting support.

Do not add potassium from a generic cardiovascular stack. The useful framework is medication-specific: identify the medicine, identify the relevant question, then build and review the support around the person.

09 of 10

Antibiotics: clearing an infection and rebuilding are different questions

The gut framework distinguishes treatment of an infection from the work of rebuilding food tolerance, bowel function and a sustainable routine afterward. A finished prescription does not tell us how the person’s digestion now feels.

We ask what changed during and after treatment: stool pattern, appetite, bloating, food tolerance and the ability to eat normally. We also consider prior courses and pre-existing symptoms rather than assigning every later problem to one prescription.

Options include S. boulardii, selected bacterial probiotics, butyrate-related support and mucosal support. Their timing is phase-specific. Their place in the plan depends on the case and the response to the preceding work.

Rebuilding may also involve gradual food reintroduction and motility work. Persistent or severe diarrhea after antibiotics needs assessment. The individual plan should explain what is being targeted, the expected response and when to reconsider, rather than promising a fixed recolonization date.

10 of 10

Thyroid medication: absorption and the foundation

The thyroid section is different from a simple depletion table. Our framework examines the medication routine, absorption, iron status, relevant nutrients and the wider thyroid picture.

Iron and selenium have different roles. Iron is involved in thyroid peroxidase; selenium is involved in deiodinase enzymes.

For levothyroxine tablets, keep iron and calcium supplements at least four hours apart according to the actual product instructions. Breakfast timing is a separate interval.

Gut symptoms, changes in the routine and other medicines can be relevant when results are difficult to stabilize. Record them before assuming that another supplement or dose change is the answer. Prescription changes remain with the prescriber; coaching makes the agreed routine consistent and follows the relevant nutrient and digestive work.
