Depleted

Medication, nutrients and the questions worth asking.

Ten medication groups reviewed individually: established effects, uncertain pairings and useful monitoring questions.

Reviewed October 3, 2026 · Educational guide

01 of 10

Statins

Our medication review starts with the exact medicine, why it was prescribed, the dose and the timing of symptoms. A drug-class label is not proof of nutrient depletion.

Statins: evidence and limits
PairingWhat the evidence supports
CoQ10Statins can lower circulating CoQ10. This does not prove a tissue deficiency or explain every muscle symptom. Supplement trials are mixed; a trial in confirmed statin myalgia found no pain benefit.
Vitamin D, magnesium, K2Statin use alone does not establish a need to replace these nutrients. Investigate relevant risks rather than assuming a class-wide deficiency.

Evidence: CoQ10 trial in confirmed statin myalgia (2015) · Small CoQ10 trial (2014).

Review symptoms with the prescriber

Discuss new muscle pain or weakness and any interacting medicines. Statins have cardiovascular benefits; do not discontinue them or substitute supplements based on this guide.

See the cholesterol guide for risk and treatment context.

02 of 10

Metformin

Metformin: separate established and proposed effects
PairingWhat the evidence supports
Vitamin B12Long-term metformin can increase the risk of B12 deficiency. A randomized trial supports this link. Ask about monitoring, especially with anaemia, numbness or other risk factors.
FolateA short-term trial found a modest concentration change. That does not prove everyone develops folate deficiency or requires supplementation.
CoQ10A routine clinically important depletion requiring replacement is not established by the evidence reviewed here.

Evidence: de Jager et al., metformin B12 trial (2010) · Wulffelé et al., short-term metformin trial (2003).

Bring dietary patterns, symptoms and other medicines to the review. A clinician may use further tests to clarify a borderline B12 result. The response should match the finding, rather than a pre-set stack.

03 of 10

Acid blockers

Proton-pump inhibitors such as omeprazole reduce stomach acid and can be important treatment. Long-term use is associated with reduced B12 absorption; the label also warns about rare low magnesium. These risks are not the same as saying every user becomes deficient.

Iron absorption may be relevant in someone with a poor response to treatment. Calcium intake and bone health should be assessed in context. H2 blockers and PPIs should not be treated as identical for every effect.

Evidence: Omeprazole prescribing information · BSG iron-deficiency guideline (2021).

Reflux is not a low-acid diagnosis

Reflux symptoms do not establish low stomach acid. Avoid using betaine HCl as a home diagnostic challenge. Ask whether the current treatment remains indicated and how monitoring fits your risk factors; do not stop or change it yourself.

Evidence: ACG reflux guideline (2022).

04 of 10

Hormonal contraception

Some combined-pill and injection studies show reduced circulating B12, but clinically significant deficiency is uncommon in the cited cohort. Findings for other nutrients are less consistent. A change in a blood concentration is not automatically evidence of depleted tissue stores.

Evidence: Berenson et al., contraceptive B12 study (2012).

The exact method matters: a combined pill, progestin-only pill, injection and hormonal IUD do not have identical effects. Review symptoms, intake and individual risks rather than taking a depletion stack solely because you use contraception. Read the method-specific guide.

Small observational studies have also reported lower magnesium, zinc or selenium concentrations in some contraceptive users. They cannot establish that the method caused a clinically important deficiency, or that every user benefits from replacement.

Evidence: Contraception trace-element study (2011).

05 of 10

SSRIs and SNRIs

A recognised risk with these medicines is low blood sodium, sometimes related to inappropriate antidiuretic hormone activity. This is a water-balance problem, not proof that the diet needs more salt. New confusion, severe weakness or seizures requires urgent medical attention.

Evidence: Sertraline prescribing information.

Routine depletion of magnesium, folate, zinc or melatonin requiring replacement is not established for every user. Nutrition can still matter, but a deficiency or interaction should be assessed on its own evidence. Tell the prescriber about supplements and mood changes; do not adjust treatment from a nutrient list.

06 of 10

NSAIDs

Medicines such as ibuprofen can cause gastrointestinal bleeding, which may contribute to anaemia and iron loss. That is a possible adverse effect needing assessment, rather than a signal to simply add iron.

Black, tar-like stool, vomiting blood, faintness or marked weakness needs urgent medical help. Tell the clinician about over-the-counter painkillers as well as prescriptions. Folate or vitamin C replacement is not automatically indicated because an NSAID is used.

Evidence: Ibuprofen prescribing information.

07 of 10

Systemic corticosteroids

Long-term systemic glucocorticoids can affect bone health and glucose. These are direct medicine effects as well as nutrition considerations; they cannot be reduced to a list of depleted vitamins.

The ACR guideline calls for fracture-risk assessment in adults taking at least 2.5 mg/day prednisone-equivalent for more than three months. Calcium and vitamin D intake should be appropriate for age and risk, with any deficiency addressed individually.

Evidence: ACR glucocorticoid osteoporosis guideline.

More vitamin D is not automatically better

A routine 5,000–10,000 IU daily supplement is not a default plan. The general adult tolerable upper intake level is 4,000 IU/day; higher treatment doses require clinical justification and supervision. An upper limit is not a recommended dose.

Evidence: NIH: vitamin D intake and upper limits.

Do not stop or change steroid treatment yourself. Ask the prescriber which bone and glucose checks apply to your course.

Some corticosteroid regimens can also increase potassium loss. The medicine, dose and other conditions determine whether electrolyte monitoring is needed. This is not a reason to add potassium independently.

Evidence: Prednisone prescribing information.

08 of 10

Cardiovascular medicines

Do not give every cardiovascular medicine the same replacement plan
Medicine typeDirection matters
Loop and thiazide diureticsCan lower potassium and magnesium; monitoring depends on the medicine, dose, kidney function and other treatment.
Potassium-sparing diuretics / ACE inhibitors / ARBsCan raise potassium. Adding a potassium supplement or salt substitute without review can be dangerous.
Other proposed pairingsDo not infer thiamine deficiency from every diuretic, zinc deficiency from every ACE inhibitor, or a need for CoQ10 from every beta blocker. Assess the specific medicine and clinical picture.

Evidence: Furosemide prescribing information · Spironolactone prescribing information.

Ask which electrolytes and kidney-function tests should be monitored and when. Bring supplement labels to the medication review.

09 of 10

Antibiotics

Antibiotics can alter the gut microbiome. In one small study of healthy men given a particular broad-spectrum regimen, much of the microbiome recovered over weeks while some changes persisted months later. This does not predict the recovery time after every antibiotic.

Evidence: Palleja et al., antibiotic microbiome study (2018).

Microbiome change does not establish a universal B-vitamin or vitamin K deficiency. Probiotic evidence is strain- and indication-specific; a generic probiotic or fermented-food plan is not automatically appropriate after every course. Ask the treating clinician, especially if immunity is impaired.

Evidence: AGA probiotic guideline (2020).

Persistent or severe diarrhoea after antibiotics needs medical review. For recurring bloating or bowel changes, the gut guide provides a record to bring to that appointment.

10 of 10

Thyroid medication

The most actionable nutrient issue here is an interaction. For the levothyroxine tablets in the cited label, take on an empty stomach 30–60 minutes before breakfast, and separate iron and calcium supplements by at least four hours. Ask your pharmacist separately about coffee and the instructions for your product.

Evidence: Levothyroxine tablet prescribing information.

Do not automatically apply that schedule to liothyronine, desiccated thyroid, liquid or other formulations. Thyroid medication does not itself prove a need for selenium, zinc or iron supplementation.

Reverse T3 is not a routine test for determining hypothyroidism or adjusting levothyroxine in otherwise stable outpatients. The claim that it blocks receptors and makes dose adjustment futile is not an established basis for care. Persistent symptoms deserve a clinical review of the diagnosis, timing, interactions and other possible causes.

Evidence: American Thyroid Association: thyroid tests.

Keep medication adjustments with the prescriber. The iron guide uses the same four-hour tablet guidance.
