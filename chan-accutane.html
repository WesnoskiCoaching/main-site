The Accutane Guide

A clearer conversation about acne, treatment and what comes next.

A guide to prepare better questions and build a more informed daily plan.

Reviewed October 3, 2026 · Educational guide

01 of 06

Start with your skin’s story

Chan’s approach begins with the wider history: when your skin changed, what you have tried, how it responded and how you are feeling now. Bring the treatments that helped as well as the ones that did not.

Record when breakouts occur, whether they are painful or leave scars, and any relevant cycle changes. These observations help a conversation; they cannot diagnose a hormonal or digestive cause by themselves.

Bring the whole picture

Include prescriptions, skincare and supplements. Keep medication decisions with the clinician who treats your acne.

Isotretinoin can cause serious birth defects. Follow your prescriber’s pregnancy-prevention requirements and contact them urgently if pregnancy is possible.

02 of 06

What isotretinoin does

Accutane and Roaccutane are names associated with isotretinoin, a prescription treatment for severe acne. It acts on oil production, clogged pores, bacteria and inflammation. It can give prolonged clearance, and some people remain clear after a course.

The decision is individual. Your dermatologist weighs the effect of acne, including scarring, against the medicine’s risks and monitoring requirements.

Read more: American Academy of Dermatology: patient safety.

03 of 06

Keep safety part of the plan

Dry skin and lips are common during treatment. Ask your prescriber about suitable skincare and report troublesome symptoms rather than trying to manage everything with more products.

Isotretinoin can cause serious birth defects. Anyone who could become pregnant needs to follow their prescriber’s pregnancy-prevention and testing requirements. Contact the prescriber urgently if pregnancy is possible.

Tell your clinician about changes in mood or other concerning symptoms. Bring your supplement list before adding anything, especially vitamin A.

Read more: AAD: side effects and skincare.

04 of 06

If acne returns

A recurrence deserves a review of the pattern and treatment options. It does not, on its own, establish a nutrient deficiency, gut condition or a hormone surge.

Bring your previous treatment dates, what changed during treatment and when acne returned. Ask your dermatologist whether maintenance treatment or another approach is appropriate.

Questions worth asking

What are we trying to improve? What should I expect next? Which symptoms should prompt a call? When should we review the plan?

Read more: AAD: isotretinoin questions and answers.

05 of 06

Make the review useful

For your next appointment, make a short record of your skincare routine, medications, supplements and previous treatments. Note sleep, food restriction and cycle changes where relevant, without assuming one is the cause of your acne.

Take consistent photos if you are comfortable doing so. Keep them privately for your clinician. Avoid changing several products at once, because it becomes harder to tell what helped or irritated your skin.

Testing should answer an individual clinical question. A broad blood panel cannot identify every cause of acne or guarantee a treatment outcome.

06 of 06

Where Chan’s coaching fits

Chan brings a nursing background and a focus on skin and female health to the wider review. Coaching can help you organise your history, build manageable daily habits and follow through on questions raised with your medical team.

Bring what you have already tried and what you want help understanding. A plan should make its purpose clear, explain how progress will be reviewed and work alongside prescribed treatment.

Individual care

This guide is education, not a diagnosis or a reason to stop or change medication. Painful or scarring acne deserves timely clinical assessment.
