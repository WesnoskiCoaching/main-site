The Metabolic Panel Cheat Sheet

Read the markers together. Know what they can establish.

Glucose, A1C, insulin and lipids can add different pieces to the picture. Here is how we use the context without turning a coaching target into a diagnosis.

Reviewed October 3, 2026 · Educational guide

01 of 04

Start with the diagnostic tests

The Wesnoski review starts with your history, sleep, food intake, activity, medicines and changes over time. Fasting glucose and A1C are established clinical tools. A fasting insulin result or a lipid ratio can add context, but neither independently diagnoses diabetes.

Common US diagnostic criteria for non-pregnant adults
TestPrediabetes range*Diabetes range*
A1C5.7–6.4%6.5% or higher
Fasting plasma glucose100–125 mg/dL (5.6–6.9 mmol/L)126 mg/dL (7.0 mmol/L) or higher

*These are commonly used US criteria; local guidelines may differ, particularly for prediabetes. Diagnosis usually requires confirmation unless the clinical presentation is unequivocal. Pregnancy uses different criteria. Your clinician interprets the result.

Evidence: NIDDK: diabetes and prediabetes tests.

Coaching targets have a different job

A narrower Wesnoski target can prompt a conversation about trends and habits. It is not a diagnosis, proof of damage or a reason to drive glucose lower at any cost. The plan should be safe for your medication and health history.

02 of 04

Understand the limits of each marker

Fasting insulin

It reflects insulin circulating under the test conditions. Interpretation depends on the assay, glucose and clinical context. There is no single universally accepted fasting-insulin cutoff that diagnoses insulin resistance. A low result is not automatically better.

Triglycerides and HDL

The TG:HDL ratio is associated with insulin resistance in some populations, but its performance varies. It cannot establish insulin resistance, LDL particle size or cardiovascular safety in an individual.

A1C

It reflects glucose exposure over roughly three months, influenced by red-cell biology. Iron-deficiency anaemia can falsely raise it; haemolysis or recent blood loss can lower it. Transfusion and haemoglobin variants can affect results too. The direction is not the same for every blood condition.

Evidence: NIDDK: insulin resistance · Sumner et al., population study (2005) · NIDDK: A1C and its limitations.

Units matter

TG ÷ HDL using mg/dL is not numerically the same as TG ÷ HDL using mmol/L. Multiply triglycerides in mmol/L by 88.57 and HDL in mmol/L by 38.67 before calculating a ratio intended for mg/dL values. A ratio is a clue, not a treatment target.

If A1C and measured glucose disagree, ask which test best answers the question before changing the plan. See the iron guide when iron-deficiency anaemia is part of that discussion.

03 of 04

Build changes you can repeat

MARCH begins with capacity: enough nourishment, a workable daily rhythm and recovery that supports activity. We choose a small number of changes, then review the response.

  1. Make meals workable. Build a consistent pattern around foods you tolerate, protein, fibre-containing foods and portions suited to your needs. Avoid an automatic crash diet.
  2. Build activity gradually. Walking and strength work can be fitted to your starting capacity and medical guidance.
  3. Look at recovery. Record sleep, energy and how you feel after training. Bring persistent snoring or marked daytime sleepiness to your clinician.
  4. Review weight in context. For people at high risk of type 2 diabetes, the Diabetes Prevention Program demonstrated benefit from a structured lifestyle intervention. That does not make weight loss the right starting point for every person.
  5. Plan around medication. People using glucose-lowering medicines may need clinician guidance when food intake or activity changes. Coaching does not set medication doses.

Evidence: Diabetes Prevention Program randomized trial · NIDDK: insulin resistance.

We do not promise a particular A1C change from a single food swap, supplement or step count. Choose a review date and define what improvement would look like in your case.

04 of 04

When the pattern needs a closer look

Bring previous results with dates and units, your medication list, relevant family history and the changes you actually made. Your clinician may look at other contributors, including sleep apnoea, endocrine conditions or medication effects. Symptoms alone cannot identify the cause.

Follow-up timing depends on the result. A1C is often useful over a period of months, but a new result in the diabetes range or concerning symptoms should be discussed promptly. Do not use an eight-to-twelve-week coaching review to delay medical assessment.

Evidence: NIDDK: insulin resistance · NIDDK: diabetes and prediabetes tests.
