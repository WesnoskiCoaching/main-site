The Cholesterol Playbook

Read the full picture without dismissing LDL.

A practical guide to standard lipids, ApoB, Lp(a), ratios and calcium scoring, with the limits of each made clear.

Reviewed October 3, 2026 · Educational guide

01 of 09

The standard panel still matters

At Wesnoski, we read lipids alongside blood pressure, glucose, family history, smoking, medications and daily habits. Total cholesterol alone is incomplete. LDL cholesterol remains meaningful even when particle-size testing has not been done.

Randomized trials show that lowering LDL cholesterol reduces major vascular events. A favourable triglyceride or HDL result does not cancel the risk associated with elevated LDL.

Evidence: Cholesterol Treatment Trialists, randomized-trial analysis (2010).

Use the panel to start a conversation

Bring the full result with units and previous dates. Ask which findings matter most for your overall risk and which changes have evidence for reducing that risk.

02 of 09

Particles, exposure and inflammation

Cholesterol travels in lipoprotein particles. ApoB-containing particles are involved in atherosclerosis. Inflammation also matters, but cholesterol does not become relevant only after an oxidation test is abnormal.

Particle size can describe a pattern; it does not make large LDL particles harmless. Measuring ApoB can help clarify particle burden when LDL cholesterol and metabolic findings do not align. Treatment decisions remain based on the whole risk assessment.

Evidence: NLA ApoB consensus (2024) · Cholesterol Treatment Trialists, randomized-trial analysis (2010).

What each marker adds

Lipid markers: roles and limits
MarkerContribution and limitation
LDL-C / non-HDL-CCholesterol carried in LDL, or in all non-HDL particles. Both are used in risk assessment and treatment planning.
ApoBA measure related to the number of atherogenic particles. Particularly useful when risk measures are discordant.
TriglyceridesHelp identify a metabolic pattern. Markedly high values require medical assessment rather than a ratio calculation alone.
HDL-CPart of risk assessment. Higher is not a guarantee of protection, and raising the number is not itself a treatment goal.
Lp(a)A largely inherited risk factor, reported in mass or particle units. Keep the original units.

04 of 09

A goal depends on your risk

There is no single “optimal” panel for every person. Someone with established cardiovascular disease may need substantially lower LDL-C than someone without it. Risk estimates, prior disease, diabetes, kidney disease, family history and preferences influence the discussion.

Our practice targets are prompts for closer review. They should not be used to overrule a guideline-based treatment goal or a prescriber’s recommendation. Ask: “What is my goal, why was it chosen, and when will we review it?”

Evidence: 2026 ACC/AHA dyslipidemia guideline.

05 of 09

Calculate the ratio in the right units

If both values are in mg/dL, divide triglycerides by HDL. For example, 120 ÷ 60 = 2.0. If both are in mmol/L, convert each before comparing with a ratio expressed using mg/dL.

TG:HDL unit conversion
Result in mmol/LConvert to mg/dL
TriglyceridesMultiply by 88.57
HDL cholesterolMultiply by 38.67

Example: triglycerides 1.35 mmol/L and HDL 1.55 mmol/L give approximately 119.6 ÷ 59.9 = 2.0 after conversion. Directly dividing the mmol/L values gives about 0.87, a different number.

The ratio has population-dependent associations with insulin resistance. It does not diagnose it or show that your LDL is safe.

Evidence: Sumner et al., population study (2005).

06 of 09

Put metabolic findings beside the lipids

High triglycerides and lower HDL can occur with insulin resistance. They should prompt a contextual review of glucose, history and habits, rather than a diagnosis from a ratio.

Our MARCH work starts with repeatable meals, appropriate activity and recovery. The metabolic guide explains diagnostic tests and the limits of fasting insulin. Lipid and glucose risks can need attention together.

Evidence: NIDDK: insulin resistance · Sumner et al., population study (2005).

07 of 09

Keep Lp(a) in its reported units

Lp(a) is largely inherited. A result of at least 125 nmol/L, or at least 50 mg/dL when that is the reported unit, is commonly considered risk-enhancing. These are separately specified thresholds, not an instruction to convert between units.

There is no reliable universal fixed factor for converting Lp(a) mass into particle concentration. Use the laboratory’s result and units. A high result makes managing other cardiovascular risks more important; it does not mean a lifestyle supplement will reliably lower Lp(a).

Evidence: National Lipid Association update (2024).

08 of 09

What a zero calcium score means

A coronary artery calcium scan looks for calcified plaque. A score of zero can help refine risk in selected people, but it does not exclude non-calcified plaque or every form of coronary disease.

It does not settle every statin decision. Diabetes, smoking, strong family history, very high LDL and other clinical circumstances still matter. A clinician decides whether the scan is useful for the decision in front of you. It is not a test to dismiss new chest pain.

Evidence: 2026 ACC/AHA dyslipidemia guideline.

09 of 09

Choose changes with outcome evidence

Consider the overall eating pattern and what replaces what. Replacing some saturated fat with unsaturated fats is supported by cardiovascular evidence. Olive oil, nuts, seeds and other unsaturated oils can fit that approach. Removing all seed oils is not established as the highest-impact intervention.

Build meals around a variety of foods you tolerate, including fibre-containing plants and suitable protein sources. Review alcohol if triglycerides are elevated. Match activity to your capacity and work with your clinician on smoking, blood pressure and medication when relevant.

Evidence: AHA scientific advisory on dietary fats (2017).

Food is part of the plan

There is no need to promise that eggs cannot affect LDL or that an over-the-counter fish-oil dose replaces lipid treatment. Review the actual response and the risk you are trying to reduce.

Build a plan around your wider picture.

Bring your results and your clinician’s recommendations. We can help with the daily nutrition, activity and recovery work that supports the plan.
