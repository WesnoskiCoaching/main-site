The Progesterone Playbook

PCOS. PMS. PMDD. Reset your rhythm.

A physiology-first guide to female hormone stability, by Farren Brown. Symptoms are signals, signals come from systems, and systems stabilize in a specific order. This guide walks you through that order.

How to use this guide

This guide is for educational purposes only. It does not diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent disease, and it is not medical advice.

Hormone health is deeply individual. If you are pregnant, trying to conceive, postpartum, on prescription medication (including psychiatric or endocrine meds), have a history of hormone-sensitive cancers, or have severe symptoms (heavy bleeding, suicidal thoughts, chest pain, fainting), consult a licensed medical professional before making changes.

If you try to "fix hormones" the way the internet does, with random supplements, random detoxes and random diets, you will stay stuck. This guide is built to do one thing: stop your guesswork and restore your rhythm.

The rule

  • Symptoms are signals.
  • Signals come from systems.
  • Systems stabilize in a specific order.
  • That order is M.A.R.C.H.

The Wesnoski rule

Most women aren't "broken." They're running physiology on hard mode: under-fueled, over-stressed, over-trained, under-slept, with low absorption, poor clearance and inconsistent ovulation.

So progesterone gets blamed, but progesterone is usually the downstream victim.

We don't chase hormones first. We restore the conditions that allow hormones to work.

The M.A.R.C.H. Method

M.A.R.C.H. is the sequencing method we use inside Wesnoski Coaching to rebuild biology predictably.

M: Mitochondria

Energy production, metabolic flexibility, oxygen utilization, cellular output.

A: Absorption & Detox

Stomach acid, bile flow, gut integrity, elimination, estrogen clearance terrain.

R: Resilience

Nervous system capacity, HPA-axis rhythm, stress response, sleep architecture.

C: Cycle & Signal

Ovulation quality, luteal strength, progesterone output, symptom stability.

H: Healthspan & Hypertrophy

Strength, body composition, performance, long-term metabolic protection.

If you skip the order, you pay. You can't "Cycle & Signal" your way out of poor mitochondria and weak absorption.

Quick start: the 7-day rhythm reset

This guide is not a personalized protocol, but you still need action. These are the highest-leverage moves we start most women with.

Non-negotiable inside Wesnoski Coaching: waking temperature tracking. If you don't measure it, you're guessing.

How to take waking temps

  • Use an oral digital thermometer and keep it by the bed.
  • Take the reading before you get up: no bathroom, no water, no movement.
  • Take it at the same time each morning as best as possible.
  • Log it daily and watch the trend, not one random day.

Morning anchor (daily)

  • Get outdoor light within 30 minutes of waking, even when cloudy.
  • Eat within 60 minutes of waking.
  • Delay caffeine 60–90 minutes.

Blood sugar anchor (daily)

  • Build every meal with protein first and carbs last.
  • Add a 10-minute walk after your biggest carb meal.

Sleep anchor (daily)

  • Same sleep window 7 days straight.
  • No screens 30 minutes pre-bed, or blue blockers.
  • Warm shower and a dark room.

Digestion anchor (daily)

  • Warm meals for 7 days. Reduce cold smoothies.
  • No large drinks with meals. Sip only.
  • Aim for 1–2 complete bowel movements a day.

What to track for 7 days

  • Waking oral temperature (mandatory): take it immediately on waking, before getting up, drinking or talking. Track the trend day to day.
  • Resting pulse (AM)
  • Sleep quality (1–10)
  • Bloating (AM and PM)
  • Bowel movements (frequency and Bristol type)
  • Luteal symptoms, if applicable: anxiety, cravings, 2–4 a.m. waking

Optional but powerful add-on if you want precision: take a post-breakfast temperature 45–60 minutes after eating to see if your metabolism is responding to food.

If those basics improve, you're in the right phase. If they do not improve, you're likely skipping the phase you actually need.

Body temperature readings and what they mean

Inside Wesnoski Coaching, waking oral temperature is mandatory. It tells us if your system is trending toward output or conservation.

The three readings we use

  • Waking oral temp (°F): baseline output overnight.
  • Post-breakfast temp (°F, optional): take it 45–60 minutes after breakfast to see fuel response.
  • Luteal shift (cycle-specific): sustained rise after ovulation.

Quick interpretation

  • Low trend, under 97.6°F: low output, under-fueling, thyroid conversion drag or high stress load. Start in M Phase.
  • Stable trend, 97.6–98.2°F: foundation is improving. Progress the plan as tolerated.
  • High trend, over 98.2°F without illness: sympathetic overdrive, inflammation or poor recovery. Tighten R Phase (sleep, stress, recovery).
  • Erratic swings of 0.6°F or more day to day: inconsistent sleep, fueling, alcohol, dehydration or stress spikes. Fix consistency before supplements.

Post-breakfast signal

  • A rise of +0.3 to +0.6°F is a good metabolic response to food.
  • No rise (flat): tighten M and A fundamentals (fuel, digestion, bile, rhythm).

Luteal rise

After ovulation, you want a sustained baseline rise of about +0.5 to +1.0°F.

  • No sustained rise: likely an anovulatory cycle or weak progesterone output, usually because M, A and R aren't stable yet.
  • Rise then early drop: short luteal phase or low sustainability, often blood sugar and stress driven.

Temp and pulse triage

  • Low temp and higher pulse: stress chemistry winning (sympathetic drive with low output).
  • Low temp and low pulse: conservation state, often under-fueling.
  • Higher temp and higher pulse: overreaching or poor recovery.
  • Stable temp and stable pulse: foundation stabilizing.

Red flags

  • 100.4°F or higher, or infection symptoms: do not self-manage.
  • Severe bleeding or pain, fainting, chest pain or severe mood symptoms: urgent care.

Phase starters: the three moves that matter

M: Mitochondria (energy)

Do these first if you're cold, flat, crashy or caffeine-dependent.

  • Protein-forward breakfast. Do not start the day with only coffee.
  • Zone 2 or easy cardio 2–4 times per week. Build the aerobic base before more HIIT.
  • Strength training: keep intensity, reduce junk volume. Sessions should be recoverable.

Metric to watch: morning energy and resting pulse trend. Both should stabilize.

A: Absorption & Detox (gut, bile, elimination)

Do these first if you're bloated, constipated, refluxy or reactive.

  • Meal spacing: 3–4 hours between meals. Stop constant grazing.
  • Bitters and salt with meals to support digestive signaling.
  • Daily bowel rhythm: same time each morning, feet elevated, no rushing.

Metric to watch: bloating resolves by bedtime most days, and stool quality sits at Bristol 3–4.

R: Resilience (stress and sleep chemistry)

Do these first if you're wired at night or waking at 2–4 a.m.

  • Protect carbs at dinner. Many women sleep better with carbs in the evening.
  • Front-load stress: hard conversations and work early, not at night.
  • 10 minutes of downshift daily: nasal breathing, legs up or a slow walk.

Metric to watch: sleep latency and nighttime wakes. Both should reduce.

C: Cycle & Signal (ovulation and luteal stability)

Do these once M, A and R are stable.

  • Track ovulation: LH strips and cervical signs, then confirm with symptoms and temp trend.
  • Luteal guardrails: dinner carbs, consistent bedtime, a walk after dinner.
  • Training edits in luteal: keep lifting, reduce maximal intensity if recovery drops.

Metric to watch: luteal length of 11–14 days, reduced spotting and reduced PMS intensity.

H: Healthspan & Hypertrophy (performance without breaking rhythm)

Do these when your cycle has been stable for 2 or more cycles.

  • Progressive strength with recoverable volume, 2–4 days per week.
  • A small deficit only if symptoms stay stable. No crash dieting.
  • Cycle-safe conditioning: Zone 2 base and minimal HIIT.

Metric to watch: cycle regularity stays stable while strength trends up.

If any phase actions worsen sleep, cravings, constipation or your cycle, that's not a "push harder" sign. That's a sequencing sign.

The cycle truth: ovulation and progesterone

The #1 misunderstanding

People think hormones are a simple "estrogen peak then progesterone peak." Reality:

  • Progesterone only rises meaningfully after ovulation.
  • No ovulation means no progesterone output.
  • Estrogen can rise again in the luteal phase.
  • Many symptoms are ratio problems, not single-hormone problems.

Translation: you can have "normal estrogen" and still feel wrecked if progesterone output is low, clearance is poor and stress chemistry is loud.

Ovulation is the event

Progesterone is not a "random hormone." It's the body's confirmation signal that ovulation happened. If ovulation is inconsistent because of low energy availability, stress load, blood sugar volatility, thyroid downshift, or gut and clearance issues, then progesterone is automatically weak.

So symptoms show up: anxiety, insomnia, breast tenderness, water retention, cravings, spotting and a short luteal phase.

What progesterone actually does

Progesterone is protective and stabilizing, especially for the brain and sleep rhythm. In practice, progesterone stability is linked to:

  • calmer nervous system tone
  • deeper sleep architecture
  • steadier mood
  • improved luteal phase integrity
  • a more stable temperature pattern

What low progesterone often feels like

  • luteal anxiety or intrusive thoughts
  • 2–4 a.m. waking
  • breast tenderness
  • irritability or emotional volatility
  • spotting before your period
  • cramps that escalate
  • cravings and appetite instability

"Low progesterone" is often not the root cause. It's the outcome of a system that isn't safe enough to ovulate consistently.

The four things that control progesterone output

  • Energy availability
  • Mitochondrial output
  • Thyroid conversion
  • Stress chemistry (cortisol and GABA tone)

Progesterone is downstream of all four.

PMS, PMDD and the estrogen–histamine loop

PMS is not a personality problem

PMS is typically a sign of:

  • weak luteal output
  • poor estrogen clearance
  • blood sugar volatility
  • inflammatory or histamine terrain
  • low nervous system buffering capacity

A note on PMDD

PMDD often behaves like high sensitivity to normal cycle shifts, especially when GABA tone is low, serotonin stability is low, stress load is high and sleep is fragile.

So the fix isn't "try harder." The fix is to build buffering capacity through M.A.R.C.H.

The estrogen–histamine loop

This loop is common with luteal anxiety, headaches, itching or flushing, breast tenderness, and bloating with constipation. The concept:

  • Estrogen can increase histamine signaling.
  • Histamine can amplify estrogen activity.
  • Poor bile flow and poor elimination tighten the loop.

So people keep "detoxing" and making it worse.

Wesnoski rule: clearance is not a hack. It's a phase.

PCOS: one label, multiple root patterns

PCOS is not one condition. It's a symptom cluster. These are the four patterns we screen for.

Insulin-driven PCOS

Clues: cravings, weight gain, energy crashes, acne and irregular cycles.

Focus: metabolic flexibility, training output protection and blood sugar stability.

Inflammatory PCOS

Clues: bloating, food reactions, skin inflammation, normal weight but high symptoms.

Focus: gut terrain, bile flow and elimination consistency.

Adrenal-stress PCOS

Clues: high stress, sleep disruption, anxiety and cycle unpredictability.

Focus: the resilience phase, circadian anchors, mineral sufficiency and recovery.

Post-pill PCOS

Clues: symptoms after stopping hormonal birth control, acne or hair changes, irregular cycles.

Focus: do not crash diet. Rebuild M and A first, then cycle and signal restoration.

PCOS is not fixed by one supplement. It's fixed by identifying the root pattern and executing the correct phase.

Hormonal birth control and the post-pill roadmap

What hormonal birth control changes

  • It suppresses ovulation.
  • It alters endogenous hormone production.
  • It changes tissue signaling.
  • It can affect nutrient status, gut terrain, thyroid signaling and clearance load.

Common rebound patterns post-pill

  • acne
  • hair shedding
  • irregular cycles
  • mood changes
  • low libido
  • bloating and constipation

The mistake most women make

Treating post-pill recovery like fat loss: under-eating, over-training, high stress and poor sleep. That keeps the rebound alive.

Recovery requires sequencing. We run post-pill recovery through M.A.R.C.H.

The post-pill roadmap

  • Phase M (foundation): stabilize energy and output.
  • Phase A (absorption and clearance): stomach acid, bile flow, elimination, micronutrient restoration.
  • Phase R (resilience): sleep architecture, stress response, recovery capacity.
  • Phase C (cycle and signal): cycle tracking, ovulation support, luteal stability.
  • Phase H (healthspan and hypertrophy): build strength, stable body composition and long-term metabolic protection without sacrificing cycle integrity.

If you want the full implementation, that's the "how" inside coaching. This guide is the "what" and the "why."

Upstream hormones and the real starting point

Pregnenolone and DHEA

Pregnenolone is upstream. It can convert into multiple downstream hormones. DHEA often reflects adrenal reserve, stress adaptation capacity and aging-related shifts.

Wesnoski rule: upstream hormones don't replace foundations. If sleep, blood sugar, thyroid function and digestion are unstable, the body will prioritize survival chemistry first.

Do not self-experiment if you have:

  • pregnancy or are trying to conceive
  • hormone-sensitive conditions
  • bipolar disorder
  • a complex psychiatric or endocrine medication history

Mitochondria

Mitochondria are the engine. When output drops, thyroid conversion drops, digestion slows, stress tolerance drops, hormone output becomes unreliable and symptoms become louder.

Metabolic flexibility

This is what we're building: the ability to use glucose for performance, use fat at rest, avoid energy crashes, and stabilize appetite and mood.

Signs you're not flexible yet

  • hunger every few hours
  • afternoon crashes
  • heavy caffeine reliance
  • sleep fragmentation
  • cravings that feel "urgent"

This is why M comes first in M.A.R.C.H.

Labs, tracking and your self-audit

Stop guessing. We pair cycle tracking, symptom patterning, labs and biofeedback markers.

Key lab categories we use

  • Thyroid: TSH, FT3, FT4, rT3, antibodies, ferritin
  • HPA axis: AM cortisol, DHEA-S
  • Female hormones: estradiol, progesterone (timed properly), LH/FSH, SHBG, testosterone, prolactin
  • Metabolic terrain: fasting insulin, A1C, TG/HDL, ApoB (when appropriate)

Critical timing note: progesterone should be assessed relative to ovulation, not a generic "day 21."

Which phase are you in? Pick the pattern that fits you.

You need M (Mitochondria) if

  • cold hands and feet
  • low morning temperature
  • flat fatigue
  • stalled fat loss despite effort
  • reliance on caffeine

You need A (Absorption & Detox) if

  • bloating after meals
  • reflux
  • constipation
  • nausea
  • food sensitivity escalation

You need R (Resilience) if

  • wired at night
  • 2–4 a.m. waking
  • anxiety spikes
  • poor stress tolerance
  • low HRV trend or high resting pulse trend

You need C (Cycle & Signal) if

  • short luteal phase
  • spotting
  • PMS spikes predictably
  • acne or hair changes around your cycle
  • inconsistent ovulation

You need H (Healthspan & Hypertrophy) if

  • your cycle is stable and predictable for 2 or more cycles
  • sleep is consistently restorative most nights
  • digestion and bowel rhythm are steady most days
  • training feels recoverable, with no lingering soreness or crashy fatigue
  • you want body recomposition, strength progression or performance without disrupting hormones

Most women need M, then A, then R before C works. Once C is stable, H becomes safe. If H-phase training makes your sleep, cravings or cycle worse, you don't "push harder." You step back to the phase you skipped.
