Why Most Men Aren't Ready for TRT

Low energy, stubborn belly fat, no drive in the gym, poor recovery. These point toward hormones, but hormones don't work in isolation. They sit downstream of your gut, liver, inflammation and nutrient status.

Start TRT without fixing that foundation and you can amplify the same problems. This guide covers what labs to run, what your GI-MAP shows, and what to address before or alongside any hormone therapy.

How to use this guide

Run your labs first. Then review your GI-MAP. Then build your protocol from the support sections with your provider. The goal isn't to avoid TRT. It's to make sure your body is ready to respond to it.

The GI-MAP

The GI-MAP is a DNA-based stool test that shows your gut bacteria, gut lining health and how well your gut is clearing hormones. Run it before any TRT decision.

The Estrobolome

The estrobolome is the group of gut bacteria that handle estrogen. When it's off, estrogen your liver tried to get rid of can come right back:

  1. Liver packages estrogen
  2. Sends it to the gut
  3. High beta-glucuronidase unpacks it
  4. Estrogen is reabsorbed
  5. Estrogen load builds

Markers That Affect Hormones

MarkerWhat It Means for Hormones
Beta-glucuronidaseA key hormone marker. High levels unpack estrogen in the gut so it's reabsorbed. Driven by dysbiosis, low fiber, low plant variety and constipation.
Secretory IgAYour gut's front-line immune defense. Low levels often show up with chronic stress.
CalprotectinGut inflammation. Hurts absorption of zinc, magnesium and B vitamins.
ElastasePancreatic enzyme output. Low = poor protein and fat digestion.
SteatocritFat in stool. High = poor fat absorption, including vitamins A, D, E and K.
Zonulin (add-on)A leaky gut marker. Useful, but stool zonulin results should be read with caution.

Bacteria to watch

H. pylori: can lower stomach acid and impair B12 absorption

Klebsiella and Citrobacter: can add to beta-glucuronidase and inflammation

Bacteroides fragilis: linked to gut lining damage

Candida / yeast: a dysbiosis driver

Stress & Cortisol

Chronic stress and high cortisol are linked to lower testosterone, worse sleep and more inflammation.

A note on peptides

BPC-157 and KPV are popular for gut repair, but neither is FDA-approved and most evidence is from animal studies. An FDA advisory panel voted in July 2026 to recommend allowing BPC-157 in compounding. The FDA hasn't made a final decision. BPC-157 is banned by WADA, which matters if you compete in tested sports. Only use peptides through a licensed provider.

Lifestyle Foundations

Sleep

Most of your daily testosterone is released during sleep. In one study, young men limited to 5 hours a night for a week saw testosterone drop 10–15%, about the same as aging 10–15 years.

Aim for 7+ hours

Magnesium glycinate 300–400 mg, apigenin 50 mg, L-theanine 200 mg, melatonin 0.5–1 mg

Get tested for sleep apnea if you snore or wake up tired. It's a common hidden cause of low T.

Diet

Eat a wide range of colorful vegetables and fiber. Fiber helps carry estrogen out

Cut back on fried and ultra-processed foods

Prioritize wild-caught fish

Manage blood sugar

Limit alcohol. It raises estrogen and burdens the liver

Bone broth, collagen and fermented foods support the gut lining

Xenoestrogens

These are chemicals that act like estrogen in the body. Cut your exposure where you can:

Use glass or stainless steel instead of plastic

Filter your drinking water

Avoid canned foods and non-stick pans

Swap fragranced products for fragrance-free and paraben-free ones

Body Fat

Fat tissue converts testosterone into estrogen. Losing body fat is one of the most effective ways to improve your ratio.

Fertility comes first

TRT shuts down sperm production. If you want kids now or in the future, talk to your provider before starting. Fertility-preserving options exist.

The Bottom Line

TRT can be life-changing, but it's a tool, not a foundation. The men who get the best results do the work first: clear estrogen, heal the gut, lower inflammation and support the liver. Run the labs. Read the GI-MAP. Build the protocol. Then make your decision.
