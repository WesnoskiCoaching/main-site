Birth Control and Your Body

The method matters. So does your experience.

Ovulation, bleeding, nutrient questions and planning a change, without assuming every contraceptive has the same effects.

Reviewed October 3, 2026 · Educational guide

01 of 06

Start with the exact method

The Wesnoski review records the product, how long you have used it, why you chose it and what changed. Contraception can provide important benefits. Different methods affect ovulation and bleeding differently.

Contraceptive methods are not interchangeable
MethodImportant distinction
Combined pillEstrogen plus progestin generally suppresses ovulation. A scheduled withdrawal bleed is not proof of a spontaneous ovulatory cycle.
Progestin-only pillNorethindrone or norgestrel pills suppress ovulation in only some cycles; drospirenone relies more consistently on ovulation suppression. Follow the instructions for the exact pill.
Hormonal IUDActs mainly within the uterus. Many users still ovulate, even if bleeding becomes light or absent.
DMPA injectionSuppresses ovulation. Return to ovulation and fertility may be delayed after the last injection.

Evidence: CDC: progestin-only pills · Mirena prescribing information · Depo-Provera prescribing information.

Evidence: Combined-pill prescribing information.

02 of 06

A nutrient change is not always a deficiency

Some studies report changes in measured nutrient concentrations with hormonal contraception. That does not establish that every user becomes deficient or needs a replacement stack.

A study following users of a combined pill or DMPA found lower B12 concentrations, but clinically significant deficiency was uncommon. Findings from one formulation should not be applied to every pill, injection or IUD. Older research on other nutrients is inconsistent and often does not establish meaningful clinical depletion.

Evidence: Berenson et al., contraceptive B12 study (2012) · Oral contraception nutrient study (1991).

We review food intake, symptoms and other risk factors before asking which tests are useful. Heavy bleeding or fatigue may justify a blood-count and iron discussion; symptoms alone do not identify a missing nutrient. The iron guide explains that distinction.

03 of 06

Record changes without assuming their cause

Track the timing of bleeding, headaches, mood changes, libido, skin symptoms and digestion relative to starting or changing the method. Bring the pattern to the prescriber. A symptom can matter even when its cause is not yet clear.

Ask what is expected for your method and which symptoms need a review. A new severe headache, chest pain, sudden breathlessness or one-sided leg swelling warrants urgent medical assessment. Unusually heavy bleeding with faintness also needs urgent care.

Keep pregnancy prevention in the plan

Do not stop or alter contraception from this guide. If you want a change, discuss benefits, risks, pregnancy plans and alternatives with the prescribing clinician.

Evidence: Combined-pill prescribing information · ACOG: abnormal uterine bleeding.

04 of 06

Build support around demonstrated need

MARCH starts with adequate nourishment, a sustainable routine and a review of sleep and recovery. We also ask what other medicines or supplements you take and whether food restriction is making intake harder.

A low test result may need confirmation or additional context. Your clinician can select treatment for a demonstrated deficiency. This guide does not prescribe a universal B-complex, magnesium, zinc, vitamin D or progesterone stack.

If pregnancy is planned or possible, arrange preconception advice, including the appropriate folic-acid plan. Contraceptive use alone does not determine your nutrient prescription. See Depleted for the evidence distinction.

Evidence: CDC: folic acid and pregnancy planning.

05 of 06

A coaching phase is not a recovery deadline

Our sequence is M for energy and nourishment, A for relevant digestive work, R for recovery, C for cycle and signal, and H for sustainable performance and healthspan. What we work on next depends on the person’s response and medical needs.

The week-by-week schedules in our coaching material are planning frameworks. They do not predict when everyone will ovulate, feel better or conceive. Some people resume ovulation quickly; DMPA can cause a longer delay. Its product-label conception data describe a group, not your personal deadline.

Evidence: Depo-Provera prescribing information.

Pregnancy can occur before the first period after a method is stopped. Plan any transition with the prescriber, including alternative contraception when needed.

06 of 06

Decide the next question together

  1. Bring the exact contraceptive name, dates and reason for use.
  2. Describe which symptoms changed and which were present beforehand.
  3. List other medicines, supplements and relevant test results.
  4. Discuss whether you want to continue, change methods or plan pregnancy.
  5. Ask what follow-up is appropriate for the method and your history.

If periods stop for more than three months without an explanation, seek medical evaluation rather than waiting for a coaching phase to finish. Expected bleeding suppression on a current method and delayed return after DMPA need method-specific interpretation.

Evidence: ACOG: absence of periods.

We can help organise the history and support the day-to-day plan alongside your clinician. We do not diagnose the cause of absent periods or prescribe contraceptive changes.
